\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \pyt{} through eight experiments, each answering one research
question:

\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{E0}] Does PyTracer\,1 still work on current toolchains
  (the motivation for the rewrite)? (\S\ref{sec:eval-v1})
\item[\textbf{E1}] Does \pyt{} detect and correctly rank classical
  numerical pathologies? (\S\ref{sec:eval-gallery})
\item[\textbf{E2}] What does each instrumentation tier cost?
  (\S\ref{sec:eval-overhead})
\item[\textbf{E3}] What does coverage accounting reveal that a
  conventional tracer would hide? (\S\ref{sec:eval-coverage})
\item[\textbf{E4}] How do the alignment strategies behave under
  control-flow divergence? (\S\ref{sec:eval-alignment})
\item[\textbf{E5}] How optimistic is the $\sig(\mathrm{mean})$ proxy
  relative to element-wise significant digits? (\S\ref{sec:eval-basis})
\item[\textbf{E6}] Does the end-to-end workflow localize a real
  instability in a scikit-learn pipeline, and do the CI gates fire?
  (\S\ref{sec:eval-case})
\item[\textbf{E7}] Is the capture format crash-safe, and what does
  storage cost? (\S\ref{sec:eval-storage})
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Setup and perturbation model}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

All experiments ran in a single Linux x86-64 container with CPython
3.13.12, NumPy 2.5.1, SciPy 1.18.0, and scikit-learn 1.9.0 (OpenBLAS
backend). Every number, table, and figure in this section is produced by
a script in the repository's \texttt{paper/experiments/} directory,
writing raw CSV results from which the tables and figures are generated
--- the paper's numbers cannot drift from the measurements.

No stochastic-arithmetic backend is installed in this environment, so
run-to-run variability is induced by \emph{unseeded random input
perturbation}: each repetition draws a small random jitter (or, in the
pathology gallery, fresh random inputs) exactly as the project's
continuous-integration suite does. We state this plainly, in the spirit
of the tool's own metric honesty: the experiments measure the
\emph{conditioning} of the traced computations under input perturbation
and the correctness of \pyt{}'s attribution machinery, not the
rounding-level stability of a fixed input. The identical workflow run
inside a Verificarlo or fuzzy-libmath container --- \pyt{} rotates the MCA
seed per run through its \texttt{[perturb.env]} mechanism --- measures
true floating-point instability with no change to any command in this
section. Detection experiments use 5 repetitions per experiment
(20 for alignment, 10 for the case study).

\subsection{E0: PyTracer\,1 on modern toolchains}
\label{sec:eval-v1}

We installed PyTracer\,1 from its final archived revision into fresh
virtual environments for CPython 3.10--3.13, together with modern
versions of its (undeclared) dependencies, and probed four stages:
install, import, CLI startup, and tracing a three-line NumPy program
(Table~\ref{tab:v1matrix}). Installation succeeds --- the package
declares no dependencies at all, so there is nothing to conflict --- and
a bare import immediately exits because a \texttt{PYTRACER\_CONFIG}
environment variable is demanded at import time. With the shipped default
configuration supplied, the CLI starts, but \emph{tracing fails on every
interpreter}: on 3.10 the import-hook machinery trips over a removed
private NumPy attribute (\texttt{numpy.lib.format.\_MAX\_HEADER\_SIZE}),
and on 3.11--3.13 the global \texttt{builtins.type} replacement itself
fails (\texttt{TypeError: type '\_Type' is not subscriptable}) --- the
highest-risk design element breaking before any tracing begins. The
rewrite was not a stylistic preference; the original tool is inoperable
on every currently supported Python.

\subsection{E1: detection efficacy on the pathology gallery}
\label{sec:eval-gallery}

\pyt{} ships a gallery of eight classical numerical-accuracy pathologies,
each pairing an unstable formulation with its stable fix in one script:
catastrophic cancellation in the textbook variance formula, the naive
quadratic formula for the small root, running summation versus Shewchuk's
exact \texttt{fsum}~\citep{shewchuk1997adaptive,kahan1965further}, an
ill-conditioned Hilbert solve (condition number $\sim$$10^{13}$), the
finite-difference step-size dilemma, naive \texttt{expm1}/\texttt{log1p},
and expanded polynomial evaluation near a root~\citep{higham2002accuracy,
goldberg1991what}. We traced each script with 5 repetitions and full
array storage, and asked two questions: does the unstable variant measure
substantially fewer significant bits than its stable twin, and does the
amplification ranking place it above the twin?

  $\sig_{\min}$ is the minimum output significant bits across runs; Gap
  is stable $-$ unstable; Amp.\ is the amplification
  (Eq.~\ref{eq:amp}) of the unstable variant; Rank is
  unstable\,/\,stable position in the per-script instability ranking.}
  \label{tab:gallery}
  \input{tables/gallery}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{gallery}
  \caption{E1 --- minimum output significant bits for the unstable and
  stable formulation of each pathology. The gap is the detection signal;
  the ranking (Table~\ref{tab:gallery}) is the attribution.}
  \label{fig:gallery}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:gallery} and Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} show the result:
\nDetected{}/\nPathologies{} pathologies detected, with gaps from 4.7 to
32.9 bits, and the unstable variant ranked above its stable twin in all
eight cases. Two readings deserve comment. First, the quadratic-roots and
polynomial cases collapse to $\approx$0 significant bits --- total loss
--- while their stable rewrites retain 25--33 bits under the same input
noise: the formulation, not the data, destroys the precision, and the
amplification column says so directly (32.9 bits destroyed by
\texttt{naive\_small\_root} versus $-0.0$ by its fix). Second,
summation-order shows the smallest gap (4.7 bits): the workload sums the
\emph{same} 20{,}000 values in a per-run shuffled order, so the naive
running sum varies purely through rounding non-associativity --- a small
effect honestly measured (48.3 bits retained) against the exactly rounded,
order-invariant \texttt{fsum} control at the full 53 bits.

\subsection{E2: per-tier overhead}
\label{sec:eval-overhead}

Table~\ref{tab:overhead} and Figure~\ref{fig:overhead} report worst-case
microbenchmarks (median of 5 measurements) in which the traced operation
itself costs $\sim$2\,\textmu s, so per-event cost dominates: many tiny
\texttt{numpy.sum} calls under T1 (\ovTinyPatch$\times$), the same calls
through the T2 ufunc proxy (\ovTinyUfunc$\times$), large-array calls where
summary computation dominates (\ovLargePatch$\times$), an operator loop
under T3 taint (\ovTaint$\times$), and an end-to-end script under the T4
monitor alone (\ovMonitor$\times$, subprocess startup included).

\begin{figure}[t]

Three observations. The per-call cost is the summaries: each traced call
computes moments, norms, and counts over its operands twice (inputs and
outputs) --- the measurements \emph{are} the product, and
\texttt{mode~=~"metadata"} exists for users who want call structure
without them. Taint mode's \ovTaint$\times$ is by design the drill-down
tier, applied to a region a cheaper tier already localized, never to a
whole program. And the tier whose job is discovery --- the T4 monitor ---
costs only \ovMonitor$\times$ end-to-end, which is what makes the
discover-then-target workflow (\S\ref{sec:design-coverage}) practical.
Real workloads with meaningful compute per call sit far below these
ratios (the large-array workload already halves the tiny-call ratio while
tracing 200$\times$ fewer events).

\subsection{E3: coverage honesty}
\label{sec:eval-coverage}

We traced three workloads (a NumPy micro-script, an ill-conditioned
linear solve, and the scikit-learn pipeline of \S\ref{sec:eval-case})
under three configurations: T1+T2 only (\texttt{patch}), the default
\texttt{hybrid} (adds the T4 monitor), and \texttt{hybrid} plus the T5
native census. Table~\ref{tab:coverage} reports what each configuration
traced and --- the point of the experiment --- what it \emph{admits} it
did not trace.

\begin{figure}[t]

  \caption{E3 --- the scikit-learn pipeline: traced functions vs.\
  admitted-untraced numerical callables per configuration. The

  \label{fig:coverage}

The \texttt{patch} rows are the cautionary tale: with no monitor, every
workload reports \emph{zero} untraced callables --- not because coverage
is complete but because the tracer cannot see what it misses. Turning on
the monitor reveals 1--4 numerical callables running unobserved
(\texttt{numpy.mean}, \texttt{numpy.hstack}, estimator constructors);
adding \texttt{--native} shows the BLAS kernels the linear solve and the
sklearn pipeline execute below Python. Closing the loop,
\texttt{suggest-targets} proposed four targets for the sklearn pipeline;
re-running with them traced the one genuinely patchable numerical
function among them (\texttt{numpy.hstack}, 5\,$\to$\,6 traced), while
the three remaining suggestions are estimator class constructors that the
census correctly reports and the patcher correctly declines to wrap. The
report also always carries the two structural blind spots as explicit
notes: operator dispatch and compiled-extension internals.

\subsection{E4: alignment under control-flow divergence}
\label{sec:eval-alignment}

We traced two adversarial programs with 20 repetitions: one whose branch
on an unseeded random draw changes which calls execute, and one whose
traced call count itself is a random loop length, then re-aligned the same
traces under each strategy (Table~\ref{tab:alignment}).

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering\small
  \caption{E4 --- alignment strategies on control-flow-divergent traces
  (20 runs, identical trace re-analyzed per strategy). \texttt{strict}
  aborts by design, naming the first divergent callsite.}
  \label{tab:alignment}
  \input{tables/alignment}
\end{table}

\texttt{strict} refuses both traces outright, with an error naming the
exact callsite and the runs missing it --- the correct behavior for its
role as determinism check. \texttt{callsite} and \texttt{fuzzy} both
recover the aligned prefix (3 of 7 call groups on the branch program, 9
of 25 on the random loop) and report the remainder as divergent rather
than silently misattributing them --- the failure mode that PyTracer\,1's
\texttt{zip}-based merging exhibited, where the shortest run silently
truncated all others. Divergent call groups are not discarded: they feed
the divergence score that ranks control-flow-unstable functions, and the
T4 line-count table localizes \emph{where} the flow diverged even in
untraced code.

\subsection{E5: the summary-proxy gap}
\label{sec:eval-basis}

Every workload from a subset of the gallery, plus one adversarial
program, was traced twice with identical settings except
\texttt{--store-arrays}: \texttt{never} forces the
$\sig(\mathrm{mean})$ summary basis, \texttt{always} enables the
element-wise basis. Table~\ref{tab:basis} and Figure~\ref{fig:basis}
compare the two per function.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering\small
  \caption{E5 --- $\sig(\mathrm{mean})$ summary proxy vs.\ element-wise
  significant bits for the same functions under the same perturbation.
  Positive proxy optimism means the proxy overstates stability.}
  \label{tab:basis}
  \input{tables/basis}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{basis}
  \caption{E5 --- element-wise significant bits against the
  $\sig(\mathrm{mean})$ proxy. Points on the diagonal are cases where
  the proxy is honest; the permuted-output workload (red) is the failure
  mode that motivates the two-basis design.}
  \label{fig:basis}
\end{figure}

For well-behaved functions the proxy tracks the element-wise measurement
within a few bits --- and for the mildly ill-conditioned Hilbert solve it
overstates stability by 3--4 bits. The adversarial case makes the design
point: a function returning the same multiset of values in a different
order each run has permutation-invariant summary statistics, so the proxy
reports \permSummaryBits{} bits --- numerically indistinguishable from
perfect stability --- while the element-wise measurement finds
\permElementBits{} bits: the value at any given position is essentially
random. The gap of \permOptimismBits{} bits is why \pyt{} stores array
payloads by default where feasible, computes element-wise digits as the
primary metric, and stamps every row that had to fall back to the proxy
with \texttt{sig\_basis = "summary"}.

\subsection{E6: end-to-end case study --- a scikit-learn pipeline}
\label{sec:eval-case}

The case study is a standard pipeline --- \texttt{StandardScaler},
8-component PCA, logistic regression --- on a fixed synthetic dataset of
400 samples and 12 features in which one feature nearly duplicates
another (separation $10^{-4}$), so exactly one pair of covariance
eigenvalues is nearly degenerate. Each of 10 repetitions applies a
relative input jitter of $3\times10^{-7}$, chosen to be representable in
both float64 and float32. We traced the pipeline with the NumPy and
sklearn plugins, then repeated the identical experiment with the data
cast to float32 (Table~\ref{tab:case}).

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering\small
  \caption{E6 --- scikit-learn case study (10 runs, element-wise basis):
  minimum and median output significant bits per traced call, float64
  vs.\ float32 pipeline, and float64 amplification. Rows without values
  are calls whose outputs are non-numeric (fitted-estimator objects).}
  \label{tab:case}
  \input{tables/case}
\end{table}

\pyt{} localizes the instability precisely: \texttt{numpy.linalg.eigh}
--- PCA's eigendecomposition --- retains only 4.3 bits with an
amplification of 12.2 bits, and the instability propagates to
\texttt{PCA.transform} (10.0 bits). The physics is textbook: the
near-degenerate eigenpair makes the corresponding eigenvectors extremely
sensitive to perturbation~\citep{higham2002accuracy}; \pyt{}'s
contribution is that the ranking finds it automatically in a pipeline the
user did not annotate, and distinguishes the source (\texttt{eigh},
high amplification) from downstream victims.

The two CI gates then fire as designed. \texttt{pytracer check
--min-sig-bits 20 --max-divergence 0.01} exits \checkExit{} (failure):
this pipeline does not meet a 20-bit floor, and a numerical CI would
correctly block it. \texttt{pytracer diff} between the float64 and
float32 experiments with \texttt{--fail-on-regression} exits
\diffExit{}, reporting three regressions beyond its 1-bit tolerance ---
\texttt{eigh} drops from 4.3 to 2.1 bits and \texttt{PCA.transform} from
10.0 to 8.1 --- turning a precision downgrade that changes no test
assertion into a caught regression.

\subsection{E7: storage footprint and crash-safety}
\label{sec:eval-storage}

Table~\ref{tab:storage} reports per-run storage on a call-sparse workload
and an event-heavy one (2{,}000 traced calls). For the heavy trace the
append-only JSONL capture costs 6.7\,MiB while its finalized Parquet is
158\,KiB --- \parquetRatio$\times$ smaller --- justifying the two-format
design: JSONL exists to survive crashes, Parquet to be queried.

  Arrays are Zarr/npy payloads enabling element-wise significance.}

Crash-safety was tested destructively: a workload that SIGKILLs its own
process --- no cleanup, no \texttt{atexit} --- on repetitions 4--6 of a
6-repetition experiment run with \texttt{--continue-on-error}. All
\crashKilled{} killed runs retain readable JSONL captures (each missing
at most a trailing partial line), the experiment completes, and analysis
recovers events from all \crashRunsRecovered{} runs, killed included. The
same scenario under a capture format with buffered row groups would have
truncated the killed runs' traces entirely.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:eval-threats}

The perturbation model is input jitter rather than rounding-level MCA;
detection results therefore demonstrate correct \emph{attribution of
conditioning}, and transfer to MCA measurements by construction of the
workflow rather than by measurement here. Overhead microbenchmarks bound
worst-case per-event cost on one container and CPU; absolute ratios will
vary with BLAS, CPU, and workload granularity. The E0 matrix supplies
modern versions of PyTracer\,1's undeclared dependencies; a period-exact
2021 environment would run it, which is precisely the point being made.
Case-study numbers depend on the synthetic dataset's conditioning; the
scripts expose every constant for independent variation.
